Practice Problem 1: Estimating Costs

Part 1 — Correct answer: avg cost/widget per arc = 11, 16, 25
Explanation: Average the transport price per widget for each arc. Use =SUMIF to
sum widgets and costs per arc separately (e.g. SUMIF($B$2:$B$210,"Site 1",
$C$2:$C$210) for widgets Site 1 -> DC), then divide total cost by total widgets.
Repeat per arc.

Part 2 — Correct answer: fixed cost ≈ 42 (42.44), variable cost ≈ 2 (1.98)
Explanation: Run a one-variable linear regression (scatter-plot the data, fit a
line, show the equation): y = 42.44 + 1.98*widgets. Intercept 42.44 = fixed cost;
slope 1.98 = variable cost per widget.

Part 3 — Correct answer: If the model ships < 5 widgets/period from plant 1 to the
DC, it is underestimating total cost.
Explanation: A scatter plot reveals a $45 minimum charge per shipment on the
Site 1 -> DC arc (average price 11). Shipping < 5 units costs more than the $11
average (3 widgets really cost $45, but the model assumes $33). So a solution
shipping < 5 units from Site 1 underestimates total cost.

Practice Problem 2: Papper

Part 1 — Correct answer: CW 2
Explanation: Build a MILP spreadsheet model (like NERD4) with Pmin=Pmax=1 (single
CW). Minimum cost is at CW location 2; total cost $349,490.

Part 2 — Correct answer: 3 (optimal number of CWs)
Explanation: Being close to the regional warehouses favors more CWs — lower
transport cost outweighs extra fixed cost. Optimal with 3 CWs = $490,882 (CW3
handles almost as much as CW2). Relax the y-constraint to sum(y) <= Pmax=5 and add
the LOS constraint as in the lesson.

Part 3 — Correct answer: More production is allocated to Plant 1
Explanation: Sensitivity analysis — if cost estimates are wrong, production
allocation shifts. New total cost $437,073; optimal locations unchanged; it becomes
optimal to produce 1380 units at Plant 1.

Practice Problem 3: Colombian Premium Coffee

Part 0 (setup check) — values: 610000, 3, 2, 263000  (ungraded model check)

Part 1 — Correct answer: min cost 66478; 2 plants used
Explanation: Transportation problem, 6 decision variables (2 plants x 3 DCs), 2
capacity + 3 demand constraints. Optimal flows: Barranquilla(P1)->Medellin(DC2)
263,000; Barranquilla(P1)->Cali(DC3) 167,000; Santander(P2)->Bogota(DC1) 345,000;
Santander(P2)->Cali(DC3) 5,000. Both plants used.

Part 2 — Correct answer: 68079
Explanation: 12 decision variables (2 plants x 3 DCs x 2 products), 2 capacity + 6
demand constraints. Capacity applies to any product unit, so supply constraints sum
products at a plant. Handle product 2's different pallet size by halving its
transport cost, keeping the rest of the formulation the same.

Part 3 — Correct answer: 2103 (per-week cost difference)
Explanation: Add two capacity constraints (limit Product 2 from Plant 1, and
Product 1 from Plant 2). Optimal cost $70,182, so the difference vs Part 2 is
$2103/week — the cost of the plant-specific capacity limits.

Part 4 — Correct answer: Add four new constraints allowing outbound shipment of a
product from a plant only if its production line is open; AND add four binary
variables representing opening each of the two production lines at each plant.
Explanation: Model-formulation multi-select (fixed-cost line-opening decisions).

Part 5 — Correct answer: Santander (Plant 2) produces both products; Barranquilla
(Plant 1) produces only product 1.
Explanation: High fixed costs make Barranquilla dedicated (one product), while
Santander produces both.

Part 6 — Correct answer: 108520
Explanation: Add four binary decision variables (produce-or-not each product at each
plant) and four logical constraints binding each binary to that product's flow out
of that plant. High fixed costs push toward dedicated plants; type-specific capacity
constraints restrict which plants handle the extra volume. Optimal cost $108,520.

Practice Problem 4: Harley Stevenson

Part 1 — Correct answer: avg cost/bicycle = DC1: 20, DC2: 25, DC3: 20
Explanation: Use =SUMIF to sum bicycles and costs per destination, then divide cost
by bicycles. Exact integers (if you get e.g. 20.51, the method is wrong).

Part 2 — Correct answer: -2180 (model cost minus true cost)
Explanation: Insert the true flows into a network model and total the costs (do NOT
optimize): model cost = $57,620, < 5% off the true cost — a good fit. Include all
cost components so the total is $57,620.

Part 3 — Correct answer: 465
Explanation: Policy deviations cost < 1% more than following the policy (hence
common). Compare against the right baseline — the model cost with current flows
($57,620), not the true cost ($59,800). Keep conservation-of-flow constraints
satisfied even without optimizing.

Part 4 — Correct answer: 1 (DC per sales region)
Explanation: Run the optimization with Pmin=Pmax=3. Optimal is one DC per sales
region; total cost $57,155.

Part 5 — Correct answer: 2 (DCs to open)
Explanation: Part 4's optimum may ship nothing through DC2, so avoiding DC2's fixed
cost is better. Set Pmin=1, Pmax=3. DC2 (unused) should not be paid for; total cost
$55,455.

Practice Problem 5: RCH Industries' Differentiation

Part 1 — Correct answer: 1 (Factory 1 not used; expected field value 1)
Explanation: Multi-commodity transshipment (5 supply, 2 transshipment, 5 demand
nodes) — like the Week 1 model but with double the decision variables (two
commodities); keep conservation of flow at the crossdocks. Objective = $23,630. In
the optimum Factory 1 is unused, Factories 2/3/4 produce both items, Factory 5 is
dedicated to Leisure.

Part 2 — Correct answer: 2 or 3 factories fully dedicated
Explanation: Free production allocation yields more specialization (the model picks
the optimal product mix per factory). Multiple optima exist, but the optimum is NOT
flexible production everywhere — 2-3 factories are fully dedicated. Change supply
constraints to limit the sum of all units (both items) at a factory to its capacity.

Part 3 — Correct answer: 700 (units of the forced minimum / cost impact)
Explanation: Forcing flexibility raises the optimal cost to $24,930. Add expressions
for units produced of each type at every factory, then constrain each to be > 10.

Practice Problem 6: Time-Expanded Network

Part 1 — Correct answer (inventory-holding cost components): Cost of capital;
Inventory handling costs at the facility (direct labor and equipment); Insurance.
Explanation: Transportation costs live in the c-matrix (not holding costs); facility
overhead is excluded unless directly tied to inventory. Holding costs include
insurance (with depreciation/obsolescence), direct labor and equipment for handling
inventory, and cost of capital (often the largest component; revisited in Week 9).

Part 2 — Correct answer: End-of-Tuesday inventory in the NY DC cannot exceed
start-of-Tuesday inventory plus incoming minus outgoing shipments during Tuesday.
Explanation: A conservation-of-flow constraint with a time dimension.

Part 3 — Correct answer: At the start of the first day, each DC's inventory equals
that DC's initial inventory.
Explanation: An initial-condition constraint fixing beginning inventory in period 1
to the given initial inventory.

Practice Problem 7: Estimating Inventory

Part 1 — Correct answer: alpha ≈ 9.4, beta ≈ 0.59 (0.587)
Explanation: Fit a power-line to the scatter plot; the spreadsheet gives
I = alpha * T^beta with alpha = 9.4, beta = 0.587 (generous tolerance — other
fitting methods differ slightly). Value of inventory is a scalar multiplier that
folds into alpha.

Part 2 — Correct answer: single DC ≈ 3147; two DCs ≈ 4189
Explanation: Plug and chug. I_single = 9.4*(20000)^0.587 = 3147. I_2DC =
2*9.4*(10000)^0.587 = 4189 (count inventory in BOTH DCs — 2095 forgets the second).
Slightly worse than the theoretical square-root relationship.

Practice Problem 8: NERD Revisited

Part 1 — Correct answer: ≈ 6835 ($/week inventory holding cost, 3 DCs)
Explanation: Three DCs open (NA 500, SP 500, WO 1000 barrels). Estimate average
inventory per DC and sum: I_NA = I_SP = 41*(500)^0.62 = 1932.60; I_WO =
41*(1000)^0.62 = 2970.20. Total ≈ $6,835/week. (I = alpha*T^beta applies whether in
units or cost.)

Part 2 — Correct answer: ≈ 4565 ($/week, single DC)
Explanation: No re-solve needed — one DC handles all 2000 barrels/week, so avg
inventory = 41*(2000)^0.62 = 4564.81 ≈ $4,565 (safety + cycle stock). Inventory drops
from 6835 (3 DCs) to 4565 (1 DC). The square-root rule would estimate 6835/sqrt(3)
≈ 3946, so NERD runs ~15% above the theoretical estimate — estimate actual when you
can.

Part 3 — Correct answer: This solution is invalid and should not be considered; the
network cannot actually meet the LOS requirement (Min % within 50 miles >= 99%).
Explanation: Raising required LOS forces an impossible solve. The model over-serves
Worcester (390/wk vs 190 demanded) and Nashua (190/wk vs 140 demanded) because demand
constraints are ">= minimum"; over-serving is the only way to raise the % within 50
miles. Switching to equality makes the model infeasible — an illustration of the
model doing anything to meet constraints and minimize cost.

Part 4 — Correct answer: Negotiate the courier's cost per barrel per mile to $0.50
(from $0.55).
Explanation: Increasing WO capacity does nothing (capacity isn't binding); increasing
NA capacity saves only ~$18/wk; cutting Bellows Falls $2->$1.75 saves ~$125/wk;
cutting Scranton $0.75->$0.50 saves ~$375/wk. But cutting courier outbound cost
$0.55->$0.50 saves $3,300/wk. Outbound (local courier) is the largest cost bucket
(cells B4:B8) — where to focus.

Part 5 — Correct answer: ≈ 9660 (Boston DC fixed cost breakpoint, $/week)
Explanation: The Boston DC enters the solution once its weekly fixed cost drops to
about $9,660 (found by bisection from the original $11k). The model gives management
a target cost for a move it wouldn't otherwise consider — humans make the final call.
