Problem 4.1

1.1 (3 points)

The potential energy

    U(x) = A(1 − cos(αx))                                        (1)

The minimum is at the points

    x = 2nπ , n ∈ ℤ                                              (2)

For example we consider x = 0,

    d²U/dx²|₀ = Aα² cos(αx)|₀ = Aα²                              (3)

This is equivalent to the parameter k in the simple harmonic motion of a spring:
U = (1/2)kx². Hence we get the period

    T = 2π (1/ω) = 2π √(m/k) = 2π √(m/(Aα²))                     (4)

Answer: T = 2π√(m/(Aα²))

1.2 (3 points)

The transient behaviour of a driven oscillation system is described by the sum of
the steady state motion and the undriven decaying motion under damping.

[The solution reproduces the trace from Figure 1: amplitude (cm) versus time (s),
running from t = 0 to t = 300 s. It starts with large, rapid oscillations reaching
about +6 and −4.5 cm in the first ~50 s, which decay into a steady, slower
oscillation of roughly ±2 cm for the rest of the trace. A short red double-headed
arrow near t ≈ 200–230 s marks one period of the slow steady-state oscillation; a
much shorter blue mark near t ≈ 5 s marks one period of the fast undriven motion.]

The period of steady state motion is indicated by the red line, which is much
longer than the period of undriven motion (the blue line). Also the overall shape
of the transient motion is in the form of underdamped decaying motion. Hence the
answer is b,f.

Answer: (b) and (f)

1.3 (3 points)

The relation between bandwidth Δf and time resolution Δt is

    ΔtΔf ∼ 1                                                     (5)

Hence the band width

    Δf ∼ 10⁹ Hz                                                  (6)

Answer: Δf ∼ 10⁹ Hz

1.4 (3 points)

No matter how weak the electron source is, there's always interference pattern on
the screen after a long time. The answer is a,d.

Answer: (a) and (d)

1.5 (3 points)

The 2d wave equation is

    ∂²ψ/∂t² = v²(∂²ψ/∂x² + ∂²ψ/∂y²)                              (7)

The lowest mode that respects the given boundary condition is

    ψ₁,₁(x,y,t) ∼ sin(πx/L) sin(πy/L) cos(ωt)                    (8)

Insert it into the wave equation, we get

    ω = √2 (vπ/L)                                                (9)

Answer: ω = √2 vπ/L

Problem 4.2 (15 points)

(a) (5 points)

[Figure: a horizontal string runs from a wall on the left. The first mass (drawn
as a filled dot, displaced upward by y₁) sits a distance 2L from the wall; the
second mass (filled dot, displaced by y₂) sits a further distance L to the right.
The tension in each segment is labelled T, with arrows along the string pointing
away from the first mass toward both neighbours. Dashed lines mark the undisplaced
equilibrium line and the vertical displacements y₁ and y₂.]

The equation of motion for m₁ is

    m ÿ₁ = −(T/2L) y₁ − (T/L)(y₁ − y₂) = −(3T/2L) y₁ + (T/L) y₂  (10)

Similarly the equation of motion for m₁ is

    m ÿ₂ = −(T/2L) y₂ − (T/L)(y₂ − y₁) = −(3T/2L) y₂ + (T/L) y₁  (11)

Hence the matrix elements for K is

    K₁₁ = K₂₂ = 3T/2L , K₁₂ = K₂₁ = −T/L                         (12)

And the matrix M⁻¹K is

    M⁻¹K = ( 3T/(2mL)   −T/(mL) )                                (13)
           ( −T/(mL)    3T/(2mL) )

(b) (5 points)

The system is symmetric under the horizontal reflection about the center point, or
the interchange y₁ ↔ y₂. The only solutions that have this symmetry are
y₁(t) = y₂(t) and y₁(t) = −y₂(t). They give rise to eigenvectors

    V₊ = ( 1 ) , V₋ = (  1 ) .                                   (14)
         ( 1 )        ( −1 )

Plug those eigenvectors into the matrix equation

    M⁻¹K V = ω² V                                                (15)

We get the corresponding angular frequencies for V±:

    ω₊ = √(T/(2mL)) , ω₋ = √(5T/(2mL))                           (16)

Answer: ω₊ = √(T/2mL), ω₋ = √(5T/2mL)

(c) (5 points)

The most general motion in terms of those normal modes is:

    ( y₁ ) = A₊ ( 1 ) cos(ω₊t + φ₊) + A₋ (  1 ) cos(ω₋t + φ₋)    (17)
    ( y₂ )      ( 1 )                     ( −1 )

where A₊ and A₋ are two coefficients fixed by the initial conditions.

From the information of initial positions and velocities, we have equations:

    ( 0 ) = A₊ ( 1 ) cos φ₊ + A₋ (  1 ) cos φ₋                   (18)
    ( A )      ( 1 )              ( −1 )

    ( 0 ) = −A₊ ( 1 ) sin φ₊ − A₋ (  1 ) sin φ₋                  (19)
    ( 0 )       ( 1 )              ( −1 )

From the second equation we get φ₊ = φ₋ = 0, and from the first equation we can
solve

    A₊ = A/2 , A₋ = −A/2                                         (20)

The motion for m₁ is then

    y₁(t) = (A/2)(cos(ω₊t) − cos(ω₋t))                           (21)

Answer: y₁(t) = (A/2)(cos(ω₊t) − cos(ω₋t))

Problem 4.3 (20 points)

(a) (5 points)

At x = 0, the pressure is the same as the atmosphere pressure P₀, the pressure
disturbance P(0,t) = 0. At x = L, the displacement of air molecules is zero, then

    ∂P/∂x (L,t) = 0                                              (22)

Answer: P(0,t) = 0 and ∂P/∂x(L,t) = 0

(b) (5 points)

From the boundary condition at x = 0 we know the normal modes are in form of

    P(x,t) ∼ sin(kx)                                             (23)

Then from the boundary condition at x = L:

    k cos(kL) = 0,                                               (24)

we derive

    k = (n + 1/2) π/L , n = 0, 1, 2, ...                         (25)

The Fourier expansion of P(x,t) is then

    P(x,t) = ∑(n=0 to ∞) Aₙ sin((n + 1/2)(π/L)x) cos((n + 1/2)(vπ/L)t)   (26)

(The phases in each term are all zero because the initial "velocity" ∂P/∂t = 0)

(c) (5 points)

The Fourier coefficients can be solved by evaluating the integration

    Aₙ = (2/L) ∫₀ᴸ f(x) sin((n + 1/2)(π/L)x) dx                  (27)

f(x) is the initial shape given in the problem.

    Aₙ = (2(P₁ − P₀)/L) ∫_(L/3)^(2L/3) sin((n + 1/2)(π/L)x) dx
                                                                 (28)
       = − (2(P₁ − P₀)/((n + 1/2)π)) [cos((n + 1/2)(2π/3)) − cos((n + 1/2)(π/3))]

(d) (5 points)

At time t = 2L/v, the arguments

    (n + 1/2)(vπ/L) t = 2πn + π                                  (29)

Hence all the cos((n + 1/2)(vπ/L)t) in the expansion of P(x,t) equal to −1. The
configuration (pressure disturbance) is then

[Figure: pressure disturbance versus x. The curve sits at 0 everywhere except over
the middle third of the tube, where it drops to the constant value P₀ − P₁ (i.e.
the initial rectangular pulse inverted). The vertical axis is marked 0 at the top
and P₀ − P₁ below it; the depressed rectangular section spans the same interval
L/3 ≤ x ≤ 2L/3 as the initial pulse.]

Answer: the initial rectangular pulse, inverted — value P₀ − P₁ over L/3 to 2L/3,
zero elsewhere.

Problem 4.4 (15 points)

(a) (3 points)

The average acceleration

    ⟨a⃗⟩ = (v⃗₂ − v⃗₁)/Δt                                          (30)

where v⃗₁ = ω x̂, v⃗₂ = ω cos Δα · x̂ − ω sin Δα · ŷ are the initial and final
velocity. Since Δα ≪ 1, cos Δα − 1 is a second order infinitesimal quantity, which
can be ignored comparing to sin Δα. Then

    v⃗₂ − v⃗₁ = −ω Δα · ŷ                                          (31)

    ⟨a⃗⟩ = −(ω Δα/Δt) · ŷ                                         (32)

Answer: ⟨a⃗⟩ = −(ωΔα/Δt) ŷ

(b) (3 points)

The electric field generated by this acceleration is

    E⃗(r⃗,t) = − q a⃗⊥(t − |r|/c) / (4πε₀ r c²)                    (33)

where a⃗⊥(t − |r|/c) is the acceleration projected to the direction transverse to
r⃗. Hence the direction of electric field is shown below:

[Figure: a horizontal solid arrow comes in from the left to the charge at the
centre, continuing to the right as a dashed line. A short arrow labelled a⃗ points
straight down from the charge. A line through the charge runs from upper-left to
lower-right; along this line an arrow labelled E⃗ points up and to the right (away
from the charge on the upper side), and a second arrow on the opposite side points
down and to the right. E⃗ is thus perpendicular to the line of sight r⃗ and lies in
the plane containing a⃗, pointing opposite to the transverse component of a⃗.]

(c) (d) (2 points for each)

The direction perpendicular to the direction of acceleration has the most intense
radiation. The direction along or opposite the direction of acceleration has least
radiation (no radiation):

[Figure: the charge at the centre with a⃗ pointing straight down. A horizontal
arrow to the left is labelled "most intense" and a dashed horizontal line to the
right is also labelled "most intense". Vertical arrows pointing up and pointing
down are each labelled "least intense". A short line through the charge running
to the lower right (the same tilted line as in part b) is also drawn.]

Answer: most intense perpendicular to a⃗; least (zero) along ±a⃗.

(e) (2 points)

From (33) we know that the electric field scales like E⃗ ∼ 1/r. Hence the amplitude
at P₂ will be 1/2 of the amplitude at P₁.

Answer: amplitude at P₂ = (1/2) × amplitude at P₁

(f) (3 points)

The total radiation power is

    P(t) = q² a²(t − |r|/c) / (6πε₀c³)                           (34)

Hence the total energy radiated by charge is

    E = P Δt = Δt · q²ω²Δα² / (6πε₀c³(Δt)²) = q²ω²Δα² / (6πε₀c³Δt)   (35)

Answer: E = q²ω²Δα²/(6πε₀c³Δt)

Problem 4.5 (15 points)

(a) (5 points)

For unpolarized light, the intensity after passing through a linear polarizer is
I_A = (1/2)I₀. Its polarization after passing through A is along x̂ direction.

Answer: I_A = I₀/2, polarized along x̂

(b) (5 points)

After the light passed through B, the polarization vector is projected to the easy
direction of B, hence the final intensity is

    I_B = I_A cos²θ = (1/2) I₀ cos²θ                             (36)

[Figure: plot of I_B versus θ from 0 to 2π. The curve starts at the maximum
(1/2)I₀ at θ = 0, falls to zero at θ = π/2, rises back to (1/2)I₀ at θ = π, falls
to zero at 3π/2 and returns to (1/2)I₀ at 2π — i.e. a cos²θ curve with peak value
(1/2)I₀ and period π. Ticks are marked at π/2, π, 3π/2, 2π.]

(c) (5 points)

After the light passed through C, the polarization vector is projected from the
easy direction of B to the easy direction of C (ŷ-direction), hence the final
intensity is

    I_C = I_B sin²θ = (1/2) I₀ cos²θ sin²θ = (1/8) I₀ sin²(2θ)   (37)

[Figure: plot of I_C versus θ from 0 to 2π. The curve is zero at θ = 0, π/2, π,
3π/2 and 2π, with four equal peaks of height (1/8)I₀ at θ = π/4, 3π/4, 5π/4 and
7π/4 — a sin²(2θ) curve of period π/2. Ticks are marked at π/2, π, 3π/2, 2π.]

Answer: I_C = (1/8) I₀ sin²(2θ)

Problem 4.6 (20 points)

(a) (4 points)

Since the slits are very narrow, the intensity is just the 4-slits interference
intensity:

    I = I₀ (sin²(2δ) / sin²(δ/2)) , δ = 2π(d/λ) sin ψ            (38)

(b) (4 points)

The principal maximas are at δ = 2πn or sin ψ = nλ/d. The minimas are at
δ = mπ/2, where 4 ∤ m (m not divisible by 4).

[Figure: the 4-slit intensity pattern versus sin ψ. Tall principal maxima of
height 16I₀ occur at sin ψ = 0 and sin ψ = λ/d, separated by two small secondary
maxima and by zeros at sin ψ = λ/4d, 2λ/4d and 3λ/4d. Ticks on the horizontal
axis are labelled λ/4d, 2λ/4d, 3λ/4d and λ/d; the vertical axis peak is labelled
16I₀.]

(c) (4 points)

When the two middle slits are closed, the system is just a two slits interference
with slit distance 3d. Hence

    I = I₀ (sin²δ′ / sin²(δ′/2)) , δ′ = 6π(d/λ) sin ψ            (39)

[Figure: the two-slit pattern versus sin ψ. A smooth cos²-like curve of peak
height 4I₀, with maxima at sin ψ = 0, λ/3d and 2λ/3d (and at −λ/3d), and zeros
midway between them at sin ψ = λ/6d, etc. The vertical axis peak is labelled 4I₀;
horizontal ticks are labelled −λ/3d, λ/6d, λ/3d and 2λ/3d.]

(d) (4 points)

The new maximas are at

    sin ψ = n λ/(3d)   (n ∈ ℤ)                                   (40)

and the new minimas are at

    sin ψ = (n + 1/2) λ/(3d)   (n ∈ ℤ)                           (41)

The principal maximas

    sin ψ = n λ/d   (n ∈ ℤ)                                      (42)

are at same positions.

The intensity of principal maximas decreased from 16 I₀ to 4 I₀.

Answer: principal maxima stay at sin ψ = nλ/d, but their intensity drops from
16I₀ to 4I₀.

(e) (4 points)

When the width of a single slit cannot be ignored, the intensity

    I = I₀ (sin²(2δ) / sin²(δ/2)) · (sin²β / β²) , β = π(D/λ) sin ψ   (43)

The zero points of diffraction factor sin²β/β² are at

    sin ψ = n λ/D , n = 1, 2, 3, ...                             (44)

The condition which principal maxima of the interference pattern overlaps with the
diffraction zero point is then

    n λ/D = m λ/d = m λ/(5D)                                     (45)

So the lowest interference order for this to happen is m = 5.

Answer: m = 5
