Introduction & Background
Introduction to the Clean'N Green Case

This case concerns Clean'N Green Incorporated.  You will be asked to assist Ralph Goodman, VP Supply Chain, as he tries
to identify and solve different supply chain problems.  The data is (mostly) fabricated, but the concepts and the
challenges represent problems faced by supply chain professionals worldwide.
Background
Clean’N Green Incorporated (CnG) was founded in the early 1970’s by a group of college students from the University of
Vermont after attending the first Earth Day on 22 April 1970.  Earth Day has since become an annual event that brings
together people from around the world to focus on environmental reform.  The first Earth Day was held at thousands of
universities across the globe and has been identified as the turning point of environmental awareness in the United
States.   The founders, both chemical engineers at that time, felt that they had been wasting their time in school up to
that point.  In order to try to solve many of the problems that Earth Day brought to light, they all quit school to start a
company that would "do the right things in the right way."
The first product that they decided to focus on was the diaper.  Disposable diapers had been in the market since 1961
and were a huge success.  They concluded that due to their reliance on plastics, the current disposable diapers were a
huge source of plastic waste. This was the origin of the NatuDiaper, a 100% biodegradable diaper made out of natural
fibers.   During their first 10 years, the company only produced NatuDiapers, which took many years to gain traction.
Initially, the NatuDiapers were only sold in niche "Pro-Environmental" stores, but as they became more and more
popular, they found their way to the shelves of mainstream retail outlets all around the US.  Clean'N Green was now the
market leader in eco-friendly diapers.
In 2002, the two founders decided to retire back to Vermont to raise Alpacas.  They sold Clean'N Green to Multilever, a
large multi-national firm with operations in 72 countries and products ranging from consumer packaged goods to
appliances to electronics.  While Clean’N Green was kept as a separate strategic business unit (SBU), personnel from the
other SBUs would rotate in for one to two year assignments.  During this time period, CnG expanded its product line and
overall operations in order to grab more market share.  In 6 different plants across the United Sates, they started
manufacturing and selling a wide range of sustainable products, from recycled-paper towels to natural soap bars and
toothpaste.   With a wider product line, they were able to graduate out of the niche specialty stores to national retailers.
For many of their products, they were able to move out of the "environmentally-friendly aisle" to the aisle for that specific
product category.   Despite this move into the mainstream market, the company kept going to great lengths to be
sustainable regarding raw materials, processes, and operations.  They maintained their mission,core values, and
sustainability remained the keystone of their marketing strategy.
Current Situation
Ralph Goodman, the current VP of supply chain, had been in his role for three years now after rising through the ranks.
Along with Ray Miller, the current VP of Procurement, he was one of few executives in left in Clean'N Green from before
the acquisition by Multilever.  Ralph had run distribution centers, transportation, procurement, and even planning in the
25 years he had been at CnG.  His immediate boss, Jim Tarkoff, COO, on the other hand, had been at CnG for 4 months.
He had run the sales group for Multilevers toaster division and was a rising star.
Ralph always thought that Jim was a little bit of a 'hot-head' but respected his directness in solving problems.  He thought
about this while he drove his Prius to work on a Wednesday morning.

Part 1: The Transportation Department

Introduction
“We have to fire the transportation team,” Jim Tarkoff shouted as he barged into Ralph’s office.  Jim is the Chief Operations Officer (COO)
at Clean’N Green Industries (CnG), a mid-sized manufacturer of sustainable fast moving consumer packaged goods.  Ralph Goodman, the
VP of Supply Chain, was puzzled.
“Why do we have to fire the entire team?”, Ralph asked.
“They are using expedited-service trucking to get our product out to the West Coast.  It will kill our transportation budget and might dent our
bottom line.  Why aren’t they using the intermodal contracts that were put in place earlier this year?”
Looking at the numbers that Jim handed Ralph, he had to agree that something was wrong.  What normally cost $2,000 to $3,000 per full
trailer of product moving from our Chicago plants to the West Coast was now costing almost twice as much.
“You need to get rid of these guys and find a team that manages their transportation to the planned budget. Let me know by Friday what
the plan is.” Jim barked, as he left Ralph’s office in a huff. Thinking to himself, Ralph sensed that there might be more to the story but he
knew he had to come up with some kind of plan or response within the next day or so since it was already Wednesday.
Transportation
Ralph walked down to the Transportation group to meet with Jana Dillworth, the VP of Transportation.  Jana’s team dealt with all aspects of
transportation for CnG to include inbound of raw materials to the manufacturing plants, movement of finished goods from plants to
distribution centers (DCs), and in most cases, from their DC to their customer’s DCs.  While some of their larger customers preferred to
pick up their product with their own private fleet, most had CnG coordinate this movement.  These were the moves that Jim was most
concerned about.
“This is ridiculous,” she exclaimed when Ralph explained the situation.  “How am I supposed to do my job with everyone changing the rules
on me?  I am providing them the level of service they are requesting at the lowest possible cost!”
“Talk me through it,” Ralph asked.
Sighing, she explained that like most companies in the United States, CnG runs an annual transportation procurement event or bid.  In this
bid, they send out a lane by lane forecast of their expected weekly flows along with certain service standards to a pre-selected group of
carriers.  These carriers are a mix of regional and national carriers and while they try to use incumbent carriers as much as possible to
maintain continuity, they always invite a couple newcomers to participate in the auction.
Once the carriers receive the forecasted volume for each lane in the network they select which lanes to bid on and submit back to CnG a
rate ($per full TL shipment) for each of the lanes they are interested in.  Jana’s team receives all of these bids and enters them into an
optimization engine that recommends which carriers should be assigned to which lane in order to minimize total cost.
“Of course,” Jana continued, “We never take the model’s output and directly implement that plan.  There are business considerations to
take into account.  So, we run different scenarios with the bids adding constraints (such as making sure we use a mix of national and
regional carriers or ensuring a minimum awarded volume for certain carriers) or modifying the objective function (such as reducing the bid
price of an incumbent carrier by a certain percentage to favor them).  Changing prices in the objective function is only for the purpose of the
optimization not for the actual payment – it lets us take business factors into consideration.”
“I understand – but did you do something different this time?” Ralph asked.
“A little bit.  We decided to allow carriers to submit both Intermodal and Express bids in addition to the over-the-road TL bids.  For
Intermodal, the carrier would be expected to pick up the load in a truck, but they could use a rail for the linehaul and then deliver the last
mile on a truck.  For expedited service, the carrier would use a team of two drivers for each shipment so that the truck could cover almost
double the distance in a day.  Most of our larger carriers have intermodal (IM) and expedited capabilities – and we thought we would see if
it would work for us,” Jana continued.
“But IM is much slower, right?” Ralph asked.
“Yes, but it is also much cheaper – and much more sustainable – which is pretty important for us here at Clean’n Green, right?  Here, let
me show some lanes for our two Chicago plants, Alpha and Bravo,” said Jana.  “The lowest cost bids for each of the lanes for TL, IM, and
Expedited along with their transit time are here.  Notice how much less the IM lanes cost!  We went with the intermodal option for all of
these lanes.”
Jana handed Ralph a printout of a spreadsheet that listed the 27 lanes from the Alpha and Bravo plants to various customer DCs the
planned weekly volume, the distance in miles, and the cost per load and expected transit time in days for intermodal (IM), truckload (TL),
and express (X).
“Let me also show you the transactions for shipments to DC202 from last week - it is fairly representative of what we are facing.  Notice on
the transaction file we have the origin, the destination, the entry date, the required delivery date, the actual pick up date, the actual delivery
date, the mode (IM, TL, or X) and the cost for that shipment.  The Order Management System auto-populates the entry and the delivery
dates.  The fastest we can tender the load to the carrier is the day after the Entry Date, but we are being pressured by marketing and
customer service to make sure the delivery arrives by the Required Delivery Date.  So, if we have an Entry on Monday, the carrier can only
pick up the load on Tuesday at the earliest and if they have a three day transit time, that means it would be delivered on Thursday.  My
team enters the actual date the carrier picks up and delivers the shipment.  Day of the week does not matter to us, we operate 7 days a
week,” Jana explained.   “You can see that we hit 100% service level since we met each of the required delivery dates!”
“I see that,” replied Ralph.  “But looking at this week I can see why Jim was so upset!  For these 15 loads the expected or budgeted cost
would be about $22.8 thousand for the week.  But, your costs are just over $35 thousand - that’s a 50% overrun, Jana!”
“Sure, but the real question is how many delivery dates would I have missed if I had used IM as planned?” Jana replied defiantly.  “You
know I would have been fired if I missed all 15 of those delivery dates, right?”
Ralph took the data, thanked Jana, and returned to his office.  Jim was right - the transportation costs were way out of alignment from the
plan, but he agreed with Jana that she did what it took to meet the required service levels.  He knew that the Alpha and Bravo plants make
very different products.  The Alpha plant produces much lower value items where the average inventory holding cost of a full truck of
product is about $125 per day while the Bravo plant product is about $350 per day.   Still, he had a lot to think about and lots of questions.

Part 2: Customer Service Team

Customer Service Team
After reviewing the transportation data and thinking about it overnight, Ralph left a message for Lyle Hiatt, the Director for the Customer
Service team to set up a meeting.  Lyle and Ralph had worked together for years and knew each other quite well.
Meeting in Lyle’s office the next day, Ralph discussed his meetings with Jim and Jana and showed him the data for the week of
transactions from the Alpha and Bravo plants in Chicago to DC202.  “Oh, this is actually not that bad of a week,” Lyle said.  “Typically, we
have seen the volume of loads from the Bravo plant be twice the planned amount!  And Bravo is not even the worst one.  The problem, in
my opinion, is the forecast we receive from planning.”
Lyle further explained how the process worked.  His team receives the 8-week rolling forecast from the Demand Planning team weekly by
SKU.   The Customer Service Team would line up inventory and coordinate with manufacturing during the normal Sales & Operations
Planning meetings.
“Here, let me show you the forecast for two of our bigger sellers, SproutCleaner and EarthWash,” Lyle told Ralph.  SproutCleaner is CnG’s
low cost, entry level organic cleaning detergent.  It is sold mainly through discount retailers where promotions are run towards the end of
each month.  EarthWash is a higher-end detergent that is sold through niche specialty stores and is never discounted.   Lyle showed Ralph
a spreadsheet with the actual and forecasted sales for the two products from July 2018 to mid-March 2020.  The MPE and MAPE for
SproutCleaner are -2% and 13%, respectively, and are -10% and 32% for EarthWash!
“So,” Ralph asked, “A negative mean percent error means the forecast is over predicting on average, right?”
“That is right,” Lyle replied.  “We are measuring the error as the actual weekly volume minus the forecasted - it is arbitrary, but that is how
we are measuring it.  In any case, these forecasts are horrible!  This is why we are constantly having to order last minute transportation.
We are constantly being caught short - with either too much or too little.”
“Here are the forecasts that planning gave us for the next 8 weeks for each product.  To be honest, I am not sure whether to trust them or
not!” Lyle exclaimed.  “Can you help?”
Ralph promised to take a look.  He had Lyle send him the spreadsheet with the last 18 months of demand and forecasts by case for both
products and he returned to his office.  Again, he had a lot to think about.

Part 3: Product Availability Issues

Introduction
A and B are some of the star products of CnG. Both belong to the household cleaning section and are hugely popular among CnG’s
customers. However, the contribution of products A and B to the bottom line has been steadily declining during the past few years. Ralph
decides to organize a meeting with Clare Brown, the VP of Manufacturing, Ray Miller, the VP of Procurement and Mary Wilson, the VP of
Sales and Marketing to analyze this worrisome situation.
First meeting
“I have invited all of you here today because we need to discuss what is going on with products A and B” started Ralph “These are two of
our flagship products, but it seems that they are losing steam.”
“Well, demand is still strong for these two products,” intervened Mary. “They are popular, and the branding is well recognized in
supermarkets all around the country. We have made strong investments in advertising and marketing campaigns, too. If anything, demand
has been steadily growing, not declining…”
“Yes, the demand is strong” continued Ralph “but have we been able to fulfill it? We have identified a concerning number of stockouts
throughout the year, particularly for product A.”
“I can say that there is not a problem of availability of the product itself. Both products A and B are in the category of liquid soaps and we
have a very flexible production line for this kind of product. Actually, we are testing a Just-In-Time approach, and it is working very well. But,
when it comes to bottling... There we have an issue,” explained Clare.
“What do you mean?” asked Ralph. “Is there an issue with the bottling lines?”
“The bottling lines are working just fine, the issue is with the availability of the bottles” responded Clare.
“Mmmm, I believe the ball is in my court now…” said Ray. “I would be happy to increase the order quantities even further. Actually, our
suppliers prefer bigger order quantities; that is why we have been placing new orders only two or three times per year. But the supply chain
department is always complaining about "storing air".”
“We could also look for extra storing space in another warehouse, if that is a problem.” suggested Clare.
“I’d rather like to create a more flexible and responsive supply chain.” added Ralph “Why don’t you renegotiate conditions with your
suppliers to reduce the lead time, Ray? I don’t think we are getting a good deal here.”
“I'll talk to them,” responded Ray.
“Another opportunity might be rethinking the approach completely. In fact, my team has reported that many bottles for product B are sitting
idle while stockouts are happening for product A bottles...” said Ralph.
“If you are suggesting using the same bottle for both products, I am strongly against it!” exclaimed Mary “We invested a lot of time, effort
and money in creating a distinctive brand for each of the products, and the bottle is a huge part of the look and feel of each product. This
move will imply a significant rebranding cost as well as a potential negative impact on sales.”
“Are you suggesting that we get rid of some of our key suppliers? We have a relationship of years, Ralph! We started the journey towards
sustainability together in the 70s.” added Ray.
The discussion was becoming heated, so Ralph decided to call the meeting off and let everyone talk to their teams and sleep on it before
making any final decision.
The moment Ray left the meeting, he picked up his phone and called his preferred supplier to discuss the situation. Mary organized an
urgent meeting with her team to examine potential impacts on sales. Ralph decided to talk to his warehousing manager and collect more
data about bottle availability and inventory costs. Clare just called it a day and left the office; in fact, whatever decision was made, she did
not think it would impact her department much.

Brainstorming to improve profitability
Ralph’s team believes this situation is outrageous. Not having any control over the promotional periods' lengths is making a dent into the
