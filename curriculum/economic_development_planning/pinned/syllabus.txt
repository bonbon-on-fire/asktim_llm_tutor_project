MIT 11.438 Economic Development Planning (Spring 2020)

Course Info:
- Instructor: Jeffrey Levine, AICP
- Department: Urban Studies and Planning
- As Taught In: Spring 2020
- Level: Graduate
- Prerequisites: None

Course Meeting Times:
Lectures: 2 sessions / week, 1.5 hours / session.

General Description:
This course explores how local and state governments use tools to attract and retain sustainable economic development. It covers how government is funded in the United States, the development strategies localities pursue, and how those efforts are evaluated through multiple lenses — including effectiveness, equity, and sustainability. The course is aimed at the aspiring planning practitioner and is oriented toward applied theory at the local level.

Required Text:
- Andrew Beer and Terry Clower, Globalization, Planning and Local Economic Development (Routledge, 2020).

Recommended / supplementary texts (optional):
- Richard Florida, works on the creative class and the new urban crisis.
- Jane Jacobs, The Economy of Cities.
- Alex Marshall, The Surprising Design of Economies (on the role of government in creating markets).

Course Structure:
The course is organized into four substantive units, bracketed by an introduction and a conclusion.

- Unit I — Introduction: Introduction to Economic Development Planning: Applied Theory at the Local Level.
- Unit II — Lenses (Why and How to Interfere in the Economy?): market intervention rationales; the evolution of mainstream theory; the creative class and placemaking; race and equity in economic development; a class-choice topic.
- Unit III — Tools (How Does Local Government Work?): local government finance overview; property and local sales taxes; intergovernmental transfers and campaign finance; annual budgeting and procurement; capital improvement programs; tax increment finance districts, business improvement districts, and Opportunity Zones; zoning for economic development; fiscal impact studies.
- Unit IV — Strategies (Moving from Concepts to Actions): determining local advantages and disadvantages; "buy local" models; the creative class and incentives; leveraging economic development for public policy goals / value capture; infrastructure and public investment; managing risk for businesses; coordination with other planning and the role of politics.
- Unit V — Case Studies (How Does This Really Work?): Thompson's Point, Portland, ME; Assembly Square, Somerville, MA.
- Unit VI — Conclusion: student presentations.

Requirements and Basis of Evaluation:
- In-class participation (15%).
- Short assignments — professional memos and shared articles (20%). Students write two one-page professional memos (graded on a check-plus / check / check-minus scale) and, over the semester, identify five relevant publications and present their findings to the class.
- Midterm paper, approximately 10 pages (25%): an academic exploration of a single theoretical lens or a specific tool, incorporating at least one case-study example.
- End-of-semester project, approximately 15-20 pages (30%): an original case study of a real economic development effort. It requires direct community engagement — it cannot rely on online research alone — and evaluates effectiveness using both holistic and financial frameworks.
- Final presentation (10%).

Grading Standards:
Written work is assessed on: answering the prompt clearly (40%); integrating course concepts and community insights (30%); and presenting a well-structured, evidence-based argument (30%). Letter grades run from A (96-100) down through D+ (56-60).

- Writing support: the MIT Writing and Communication Center offers free consultations.
- Academic integrity: students must cite sources properly and may not submit recycled coursework. See https://integrity.mit.edu/.
