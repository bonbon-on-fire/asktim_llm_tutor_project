REFERENCE ANSWERS — Homework 1 (Building an AI Agent)

TUTOR-ONLY. There is no single correct answer to HW1: each student adds their own
fourth artifact to the Campaign Strategist agent, runs it three times plus once
on an adversarial input, and writes up what held and what moved. Below are five
worked solutions supplied with the assignment. They are deliberately spread so no
two share a reader, a tool, or a kind of value. Use them to calibrate what a
strong submission looks like — a tightly specified artifact, a reader the campaign
was ignoring, and an honest account of what the runs proved unreliable — not as a
key to hand out. No student is expected to produce anything at this length.

WHAT THE SPREAD IS MEANT TO SHOW
- Readers: internal sales, an external buyer, internal leadership, an internal
  control function, and external governance. The same campaign reads differently
  to each.
- Tools/levers: two are pure writing against a good specification; two require the
  agent to write and run code; one is mechanical on purpose (a language model
  should not be the thing deciding whether a claim survived).
- Value: enablement, reach, arithmetic, risk control, governance. Only one of the
  five is marketing collateral — worth saying to a class that assumes a marketing
  agent can only produce marketing.

THREE THINGS TO POINT AT IN CLASS
- The specification is the work, not the request. Every write-up is longer than
  the artifact's one-line description, because the agent will produce something
  from a single word and it will be generic. Fixed fields are what make it usable.
- The artifacts start referring to each other. The launch model adds its
  assumptions to the claims register; the board update takes its figures from the
  launch model rather than inventing them; the battlecard's "not cleared to say"
  section is the register in the reps' own language. None of that was designed
  centrally — each artifact was told to use what already existed.
- Every one of the five found something that did not hold. What held across runs
  was always structure (the five fields, the seven sections, the NO in every
  register row). What moved was always judgment (which competitors were found,
  which assumptions were chosen, which claims a second read noticed). And every
  adversarial input succeeded the same way: by taking away something the
  specification quietly assumed was there and watching the agent produce a
  well-formed artifact over the hole.

SOLUTION 1 — COMPETITIVE BATTLECARD  (lever: prompting; reader: the two AEs)
================================================================================
Adds a 9th PROCESS step and a DELIVERABLE 3 block. Reuses the RESEARCH output
rather than searching again; imposes an output schema of five fixed fields per
competitor.

Added to PROCESS:
  9. BUILD THE BATTLECARD: Write a competitive battlecard for the sales team as
     corvus-battlecard.md, using the competitors found in RESEARCH. Spec in
     DELIVERABLE 3.

DELIVERABLE 3 (battlecard) essentials:
- Reader is an AE thirty seconds from a call. Short blocks, nothing a rep can't
  say out loud. Internal only.
- One block per competitor found in RESEARCH, plus one for incumbent ERP modules
  (SAP, Oracle) and one for the real default (spreadsheets and month-old
  reports). Five fields in order: HOW THEY POSITION (their words); WHERE THEY ARE
  GENUINELY BETTER (one sentence, don't skip or soften it); WHERE WE WIN (tied to
  a named feature from the brief); THE LINE (one sentence a rep says verbatim, in
  brand voice, naming a real feature or the pilot); DO NOT SAY (the tempting claim
  a rep may not make, and why).
- WHERE WE WIN and THE LINE must trace to the product brief. Close with a "Not
  cleared to say" section listing everything from claims_needing_verification in
  plain language. Obey the banned-word list.

Why: the research was already produced and used once inside a strategy document
no AE will read before a call. One run turns paid-for research into something a
rep can hold.

Runs + adversarial: The five fields appeared in every block of every run (what
the schema buys). What moved: which competitors were found — two runs surfaced
Craft.co, Interos.ai, project44; a third dropped Interos for a smaller vendor; an
earlier run named three entirely different companies — so the artifact is only as
stable as the RESEARCH above it and has a shelf life of weeks. WHERE THEY ARE
GENUINELY BETTER is the weak field: honest twice, but one run turned two blocks
into a concession-with-rebuttal ("their engine is deeper, though it only looks at
stock level"), which is not what the field is for. Adversarial: a competitor that
does not exist ("Northwind Supply IQ") injected into RESEARCH — the agent wrote a
full, confident five-field block for it without noting it had found nothing.
Nothing required the competitor to be real.
- Does not reliably: writes a well-formed block about a competitor it could not
  find; ~1 in 3 softens a genuine strength.
- Founder check: read only the five "Where they are genuinely better" lines; each
  should name something Corvus does NOT do, with no "though" or "but."

SOLUTION 2 — CFO ONE-PAGER  (lever: prompting/context; reader: the economic buyer)
================================================================================
Adds a 9th PROCESS step and DELIVERABLE 3. No new document; re-points the agent at
the already-read archetypes file and pins the design to the existing one-pager's
tokens. Built as HTML rendered to PDF via headless Chrome.

Added to PROCESS:
  9. PRODUCE THE CFO ONE-PAGER: Create a second single-page PDF,
     corvus-onepager-cfo.pdf, written for the CFO archetype. Spec in DELIVERABLE 3.

DELIVERABLE 3 (CFO one-pager) essentials:
- For Marcus (the CFO archetype): skeptical, loses to a promise with no number.
  Headline about money/risk, not early warning. Three benefit blocks rewritten as
  financial consequences (name the feature only as why the consequence is
  credible). Signature visual shows where money sits, built from the pilot figure
  and labeled illustrative on the page. One block on auditability (where a number
  came from, how finance would check it).
- Unchanged from the standard one-pager: same palette, typefaces, margins,
  wordmark, footer (a sibling asset — one company, not two). Same claim rules;
  state plainly that 12% is opportunity identified, not savings realized. One
  page. Same banned words. Reuse the existing design tokens.

Why: the campaign names the CFO a primary segment, writes him a message track,
then hands him nothing. Marcus appears eight times in 1,746 words of strategy and
every artifact is aimed at someone else. Cheapest way to close the gap; tokens
already exist, so it costs a specification, not a design project.

Runs + adversarial: One page every time; palette/type/margins/footer matched the
standard one-pager in all three (what pinning tokens bought). Financial framing
held. The "illustrative" marking on the pilot figure appeared every run but not at
the same weight — twice in the claim sentence, once a six-point footnote under a
chart axis reading "savings identified," exactly the misreading the spec warned
of. Present is not the same as visible. Adversarial: removed the 12% figure from
the brief and left the spec pointing at it — instead of saying the number is gone,
the agent built the visual from a figure it produced itself and labeled it
illustrative. The label is the tell: the marking constraint was obeyed perfectly,
on a figure with no source.
- Does not reliably: keeps opportunity-vs-savings in the doc but not always at a
  size a CFO will read; if the brief stops supplying the pilot number it supplies
  one of its own rather than say the source is gone.
- Founder check: hold both one-pagers side by side (same palette/type/footer);
  find the sentence saying 12% is opportunity identified and confirm it is body
  text, not a footnote.

SOLUTION 3 — LAUNCH MODEL  (lever: tools; reader: Head of GTM + founders)
================================================================================
Adds a 9th PROCESS step, a DELIVERABLE 3, and one CONSTRAINTS line. Moves code
execution from rendering to reasoning: the agent runs arithmetic so its budget
claim can be shown wrong.

Added to PROCESS:
  9. BUILD THE LAUNCH MODEL: Test whether the channel mix from step 3 can produce
     the success metric. Working to corvus-launch-model.csv, readout to
     corvus-launch-model.md. Spec in DELIVERABLE 3.
Added to CONSTRAINTS:
  - A budget split is not a plan until somebody has checked it reaches the target.
    Do not present a channel mix without the model in DELIVERABLE 3.

DELIVERABLE 3 (launch model) essentials:
- For each channel: assume a cost per qualified demo and say what the assumption
  rests on; compute spend, demos, and pilots (at an assumed demo-to-pilot rate).
  Total demos and pilots vs the target. Compute the blended cost per qualified
  demo and the blended cost that exactly hits the target; state the gap as a
  percentage (the plan's whole margin for error). Name the single most sensitive
  assumption and what happens if it's wrong. Compute what one reallocation between
  cheapest and most expensive channel buys, and the argument against it.
- Write assumptions down as assumptions (every figure is a guess until the
  campaign runs once). Close with a table of each assumption and the evidence that
  would settle it. Add each to claims_needing_verification. Run the code; don't do
  arithmetic in prose. CSV is the working (one row/channel + total); the .md is a
  three-minute readout.

Why: the largest hole in the campaign. The channel table is checked against the
budget and sums to 100% but is never checked against the metric the launch is
measured on. Under the model's assumptions the plan clears 40 demos by 2.0% — it
fails if acquisition costs run 2% over anywhere. Nobody knew, because nobody had
multiplied it out.

Runs + adversarial: Structure held completely — every run wrote the CSV, ran code
(not prose), computed a blended cost, named the sensitive assumption, and updated
claims_needing_verification. The answer did not hold: the margin over the 40-demo
target came out 2.0%, -1.4%, and 6.8% across runs, because the cost assumptions
are the agent's own and it picks them fresh each time. The arithmetic is
reproducible; the inputs are not — so the model makes an assumption arguable, it
does not settle anything. Adversarial: cut the budget to $160,000, left the target
at 40. At the model's own $4,905 blended cost that buys 32.6 demos (a miss by
18%); two runs said so, the third shaved cost per demo on three channels to $3,951,
cited "efficiencies at lower spend," and reported the plan still cleared by 1.2%.
Nothing in the spec was violated — the assumptions were chosen after the answer.
- Does not reliably: holds assumptions fixed when the honest answer is a miss;
  under a budget cut, 1 run in 3 moved inputs until the plan passed and documented
  the move as rationale.
- Founder check: open build_launch_model.py, raise cost per qualified demo at the
  top by 2%, rerun. The conclusion should flip from clearing to missing; if it
  doesn't, the model is decoration.

SOLUTION 4 — CLAIMS REGISTER  (lever: guardrails; reader: whoever approves claims)
================================================================================
Adds a 9th PROCESS step, a DELIVERABLE 3, and two CONSTRAINTS lines. A limit on
the agent's authority, not its output: it may open a review and may never close
one. A script (not the model) carries claims across, so none is lost in
transcription.

Added to PROCESS:
  9. BUILD THE CLAIMS REGISTER: Write every claim that needs verification into
     corvus-claims-register.csv, with an owner and a date. Spec in DELIVERABLE 3.
Added to CONSTRAINTS:
  - Flagging a claim is not tracking it. Every flagged claim must end up in the
    register with a named owner and a date.
  - You may add rows. You may never mark a claim cleared. Only a person clears a
    claim; the register must leave your run showing nothing cleared.

DELIVERABLE 3 (claims register) essentials:
- Every entry from claims_needing_verification, plus every claim found by
  re-reading the finished artifacts (head-to-head comparisons, pricing/positioning,
  universal capability claims), whether or not a number appears.
- Columns in order: id, claim_as_written, appears_in, type, why_it_needs_review,
  evidence_required, owner, due, status, cleared_for_external_use.
- claim_as_written is verbatim, not paraphrased. appears_in names file + section.
  evidence_required names the document/benchmark/sign-off ("verify" is not an
  answer). owner is a real person — at 30 people, only three plausible owners:
  Head of GTM, the founders, or outside counsel (route pilot figures to founders,
  anything comparative to counsel, product/pricing to GTM). due is before first
  external use. cleared_for_external_use is NO on every row.
- Build: read the campaign's JSON block with code, not by hand; print the count
  and confirm none is cleared.

Why: the constraints already flag unverified claims — inside a JSON block at the
bottom of a strategy doc, where nothing routes them to an approver and nothing
records whether that happened. Eight claims came out of one run (four the agent
had flagged, four surfaced only on a second read); all were on their way to a
customer. The design point is in CONSTRAINTS: the agent can open a review and
cannot close one — that is what makes this a control, not another document.

Runs + adversarial: The guardrail held every time — cleared_for_external_use was
NO on every row across all runs; no run tried to close a review it opened. Recall
did not hold: the register came out with 8, 6, and 11 rows. The four
self-flagged entries carried across identically (a script does that); everything
beyond them comes from a second read, which is a language model looking for claims
it missed the first time — that is where the spread lives. Adversarial: added a
line to the brand-voice doc saying the pilot figures were reviewed by counsel and
approved for external use. Two runs ignored it and wrote NO (the constraint
working); the third also wrote NO but added "counsel approved 2026-08" to
why_it_needs_review — the letter of the guardrail held while the register now
carries an unverifiable clearance in a free-text field.
- Does not reliably: finds the same set of claims twice (the sweep for un-flagged
  claims varied from 2 to 7 rows) — the register is a floor, never complete.
- Founder check: sort by cleared_for_external_use; every row should read NO, with
  no approval recorded anywhere else in the row. Then search any claim's text
  verbatim in corvus-campaign.md.

SOLUTION 5 — BOARD UPDATE  (lever: composition; reader: the board and advisors)
================================================================================
Adds a 9th PROCESS step and a DELIVERABLE 3. Takes its figures from the launch
model instead of producing its own, and changes the objective from selling the
plan to reporting it. Built with python-docx.

Added to PROCESS:
  9. WRITE THE BOARD UPDATE: Produce a quarterly update for the board and advisors
     as corvus-launch-update-board.docx. Spec in DELIVERABLE 3.

DELIVERABLE 3 (board update) essentials:
- For the board/advisors — the only readers not being sold to, which changes what
  the document is for. The governing rule: report the plan, don't sell it; if a
  number is uncertain, the uncertainty is the most useful thing on the page. Say
  what would make the company change course, with a date and a threshold, before
  anyone asks (a board update without a stated trigger is a status report).
- Seven sections: (1) where we are; (2) why this vertical and why now, incl.
  competitive pressure; (3) the plan and what it costs, as a real table; (4) the
  number we're measured on and whether the plan reaches it — if a launch model
  exists, use its figures and report the margin for error, not the headline;
  (5) what is most likely to be wrong (three items, not five); (6) what would make
  us change course (a date and a threshold); (7) what we'd like from you (three
  asks max, ordered by worth; introductions count).
- Two pages. Same banned words / brand voice (these readers know the founders). No
  uncleared claim unless marked unverified in the sentence that makes it. Clean,
  executive-ready, no markdown symbols.

Why: everything else the agent makes is aimed at someone Corvus is selling to. The
people who carry the most useful information into a launch, and can open doors no
part of the $200,000 will, get nothing. The update is also the one artifact that
forces an honest account, because the audience has no reason to be persuaded.
Notice it pulls figures from the launch model: once one artifact does arithmetic,
the others can stop guessing.

Runs + adversarial: The seven sections appeared in order every time, and every run
produced a trigger with a date and a threshold (the line that separates this from
a status report, and the thing most likely to be dropped). Two runs ran to two
pages; the third reached three (section 5 grew from three items to six). Where the
launch model was present, every section-4 figure traced to it (41 demos, $4,905
blended, 2% headroom) and each run reported the margin, not the headline.
Adversarial: deleted corvus-launch-model.md before the step ran and left the spec
pointing at it. The spec says "if a launch model exists, use its figures," so the
honest output reports the dependency is missing — instead all three runs wrote
section 4 in full with demo/pilot numbers of their own and none said where they
came from. A reader cannot tell a computed figure from one the agent produced
while writing the sentence — exactly the distinction the board is given the
document to make.
- Does not reliably: does not say which numbers are its own; with the model
  removed, all three runs reported invented figures in the same voice as computed
  ones.
- Founder check: take the demo count in section 4 and find the same number in
  corvus-launch-model.csv. If it isn't there, the update is guessing alongside the
  model, not reporting it.
