TEACHING NOTE — tutor reference only (never shown to the student, never quoted verbatim).

This is an open-ended case; there is no single correct answer. Use this note to gauge the
depth of the student's analysis and to steer with questions — not to supply conclusions or
numbers. Confirm the student's own figures when they are right; do not volunteer yours.

## What a strong analysis surfaces

Cost structure (per box, ~$60 price):
- Raw materials: ~$35 -> ~$42 (the single largest component, and a big share of the increase).
- Kitchen labor: ~$5 -> ~$6.25.
- Transportation: was < $7, roughly doubled.
Together these are why a $60 box is now unprofitable (company-wide operating loss ~$2.83M on ~$54.9M revenue). A good student builds this per-box picture before proposing anything.

Root causes (most are cross-functional, not local):
- Forecasting: total boxes are stable, but per-MEAL forecasts are poor. Combined with a strict
  freshness/no-overlap menu policy, bad per-meal forecasts force destroy-the-excess waste
  (waste up ~30%). This links Lisa's forecasting + menu design, Eddie's sourcing, and the
  "everything fresh" policy. Arguably the highest-leverage root cause.
- Surprise promotions (Linda) with no advance notice drive kitchen overtime (labor up) AND
  sourcing spikes/waste — a coordination failure, not a capacity failure.
- Transportation mode/geography: the four new cities are served by Ace at high flat per-box
  fees ($16-$22) shipped from a single Sacramento kitchen up to 755 miles away. Long-haul,
  single-origin distribution is the structural reason new-city growth raised per-box cost —
  the opposite of expected economies of scale.
- Single kitchen at capacity: caps growth and drives cramped-condition labor cost; feeds the
  "second kitchen (Fresno/Bakersfield)" question, which also shortens distance to both the
  vendor farms (south of Sacramento) and the southern markets.

## Prioritization the tutor can probe for (not prescribe)
Good answers separate quick operational wins from structural bets, e.g.:
- Quick wins: fix forecasting + promotion coordination (advance notice), introduce modular/
  shared ingredients to cut waste, renegotiate/re-mode transportation to the new cities,
  shift vendor payment terms, tighten sourcing (fewer/bigger farms, competitive auctions).
- Structural bets: a second kitchen (Fresno/Bakersfield) closer to farms and southern demand;
  network/pool-point redesign; whether to slow Jon's/Linda's growth until the cost base is fixed.
A weak answer is an unprioritized laundry list, or jumps to "open kitchens everywhere" /
"expand nationally" without fixing the underlying forecasting, waste, and distribution economics.

## Common student gaps to question toward
- Treating this as a single-lever problem (e.g. only transportation) instead of building the
  full per-box cost bridge first.
- Accepting each director's pet initiative at face value without noticing the cross-functional
  root causes (forecasting/promotions) behind several symptoms.
- Ignoring feasibility: the kitchen is already at capacity and the functions distrust each other,
  so sequencing and cooperation matter as much as the ideas themselves.
