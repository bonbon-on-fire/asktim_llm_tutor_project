Course Content and Objectives:
For over a century, statistics has taken probability models as a primitive assumption. In today's data-rich world that assumption is often unnecessary and can lead to inaccurate predictions and suboptimal decisions. The majority of central ML problems — Lasso for sparse regression, CART for classification and regression, among many others — have been addressed with heuristic methods even though they can be formulated as formal optimization problems. This course provides a unified, insightful, and modern treatment of ML using three modern optimization lenses: convex, robust, and mixed-integer optimization. We de-emphasize probability models, start with data, and revisit the central problems of ML using formal optimization, demonstrating that they benefit greatly from a modern optimization treatment. Specifically, we show that using modern optimization we can find solutions to large-scale instances of central ML problems that (a) can be found in seconds/minutes, (b) can be certified optimal in minutes/hours, and (c) outperform classical heuristic approaches out of sample on real-world and synthetic datasets. We also review developments in deep learning for prediction and prescription, multimodal AI, and explainable multimodal AI as ways to overcome the bias in observational data.

Guiding Philosophy:
(a) Interpretability. Interpretability in ML matters, especially in critical applications; the course emphasizes sparsity for interpretable regression and classification, optimal trees, prescriptive and policy trees, stable regression, interpretable multimodal AI, interpretable clustering, interpretable matrix completion, and interpretable optimization.
(b) The link between ML and optimization. Linking ML/statistics to optimization advances our ability to solve these problems and provides a fresh perspective; Lecture 23 explores the reverse direction, using ML to give interpretability to optimization.
(c) Robustness is more important than optimality. A robust solution is preferable to a brittle optimal one.
(d) Randomization versus optimization. Optimal classification and regression trees are shown to match random forests in performance.
(e) Practability. Methods are judged by their ability to solve problems at sizes and in times appropriate to the application, not by polynomial solvability or NP-hardness.
(f) Prescriptive methods. The ultimate objective is high-quality decisions, not just prediction.
(g) Deep Learning and Multimodal ML. Deep learning and multimodal ML combine structured and unstructured data for prediction.
(h) Debiasing observational data. Debiasing enables personalized medicine on both observational data and randomized trials.
